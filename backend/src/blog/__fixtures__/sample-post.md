
To organise a hackathon in college, lock the date, venue and a small core team 4 to 9 months out, open registrations about 3 months before, book judges and mentors 2 months before, and plan for roughly half of your registrants to turn up. That timeline comes from the Major League Hacking (MLH) organiser guide, the closest thing student hackathons have to a shared playbook [1]. Below it's turned into the decisions you'll actually have to make, with the numbers that matter for judging and headcount.

This is for student clubs in India running a first or second hackathon of 50 to 500 people. If you're running your college's Smart India Hackathon internal round, skip to that section; the rules are different.

## The timeline, month by month

| When | What gets done |
|---|---|
| 4 to 9 months before | Date and purpose, leadership team, venue |
| 4 months before | Placeholder website, budget, sponsorship prospectus, sponsor outreach |
| 3 months before | Promotion starts, main website goes live, registrations open |
| 2 months before | Judging and mentorship plans, judges and mentors booked, food vendors, workshops |
| 1 month before | Detailed run of show, final headcount, dietary needs, transport |
| 1 week before | Reminders to participants, venue setup, emergency plan, team run-through |

Source: MLH Hackathon Organizer Guide [1].

The first row's 4 to 9 month range is wide on purpose. A lab block during placement season can be booked out a semester ahead; a seminar hall early in term might be free next month. Get the venue first and let it set the date.

Approvals, budget sheets and the core team work the same way they do for a fest, so there's no need to reinvent them. Our [8-week college fest checklist](/blog/how-to-organize-a-college-fest) covers that part week by week.

## Pick the format

Most student hackathons run for 24 to 48 hours [2]. Our view: make your first one 24 hours. The second night costs you volunteers, a second round of meals and a tired judging session, and first-time teams rarely use the extra hours well. Move to 36 once your team has run one before.

Two more calls to make early, because everything downstream depends on them:

- **Problem statements or open theme.** Statements give sponsors something to attach a prize to. Open themes get more varied projects.
- **Team size.** Fix a range, put it in the written rules and enforce it at registration. MLH's guide makes the same point: rules written clearly up front save arguments later [3].

## Plan for half your sign-ups to show

MLH puts turnout at about 50% of registrants and tells organisers to aim for double the registrations they want attendees, but never more than double [4]. A 150-seat venue means capping registrations at 300.

Keep a waitlist and promote from it as people drop out. MLH suggests giving waitlist priority to people who live close to the venue, since they're more likely to come [4]. Its checklist also puts participant reminders in the final week [1].

## Judging

MLH recommends a science fair format: teams stay at their tables and judges walk round [3]. It recommends against separate judging rooms, because each team is only compared with the projects in its own room, and that makes a fair overall ranking hard.

### How many judges you need

The guide's formula is:

**judges = (projects × rounds × minutes per project) ÷ total judging minutes**, rounded up.

Its defaults are 3 rounds per project, so every project is seen by 3 different judges, and 4 minutes per project: 2 minutes to present and demo, 1 for questions and scoring, 1 for the judge to walk to the next table [3]. MLH's own example is 175 projects, 120 minutes of judging, 3 rounds and 4 minutes each, which comes to 17.5, so 18 judges.

Most college hackathons are smaller. MLH's 2026 season averaged about 1 project for every 4 checked-in hackers [3], so 200 people checked in means roughly 50 projects. At 3 rounds, 4 minutes and 120 minutes, that's (50 × 3 × 4) ÷ 120 = 5 judges. We'd book one or two extra, in case someone drops out the night before.

Leave 2 to 3 hours in the schedule for judging; that's the buffer MLH recommends [3].

### What judges score

Keep the rubric short. One hackathon playbook suggests 4 to 6 criteria at most [5]. Two real examples:

- Okta's 2021 hackathon, run on Devpost, used three criteria: creativity and originality, implementation, and potential impact [6].
- A Claremont Graduate University rubric scores each criterion from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) and gives each a percentage weight, for example 20% for socially responsible impact [7].

Hand teams the rubric at the opening ceremony and give judges the same sheet. If a sponsor is funding a track prize, agree in writing whether their track uses your main rubric or their own.

## Running your college's SIH internal hackathon

Colleges use the internal hackathon to choose which teams get nominated to Smart India Hackathon. Only teams that clear internal screening go forward [8]. For the 2026 edition, college pages list these team rules [8]:

- Exactly 6 students, including the team leader
- At least 1 female student per team
- All members from the same institution; teams mixing branches of that institution are encouraged [9]

Rules change between editions, so confirm them on [sih.gov.in](https://sih.gov.in) and with your college's SPOC before you publish yours. In practice, your registration form has to enforce the 6-member and gender rules. If it doesn't, you'll be turning teams away by hand the night before.

## On the day

MLH's game-day list is short: check-in, meals, judging, mentorship, workshops, and the opening and closing ceremonies [1]. The emergency plan from the week before should already say who handles a medical issue, a power cut or a lost laptop.

Two things make the 24 hours calmer:

1. **A help desk with named owners.** Teams will need Wi-Fi, an extension board, a mentor for a specific stack or a ruling on the rules. Put one person on each kind of question rather than one shared phone number.
2. **One announcement channel.** Every schedule change goes out once, in one place, at the time it's decided.

## What this guide can't tell you

The 50% turnout figure and the 1-project-per-4-hackers ratio both come from MLH, whose member events are mostly free and open to anyone. We haven't found published turnout numbers for Indian college hackathons. If yours charges a registration fee or teams are nominated by their department, turnout may be quite different. Count it at your first event and plan the second one with your own number.

## FAQ

### How long should a college hackathon be?

Most run 24 to 48 hours. For a first event, 24 hours is easier to staff and feed.

### How many registrations should we accept?

Up to double your venue capacity, and no more. MLH's guide puts turnout at about half of registrants.

### How many judges does a hackathon need?

Multiply projects by 3 rounds and 4 minutes, then divide by your judging minutes. 50 projects in a 2-hour window needs 5 judges.

### When should we contact sponsors?

MLH's timeline has the budget, the sponsorship prospectus and sponsor outreach all starting about 4 months before the event.

### What are the team rules for an SIH internal hackathon?

For 2026, college pages list exactly 6 students per team, at least 1 of them female, all from the same institution. Check sih.gov.in for the current edition.
